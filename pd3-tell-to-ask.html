From Tell to Ask

Asking students what they think before showing them what others think
Four modules · 6 hours · self-paced · portfolio-assessed

Before you start

PD 1 and PD 2 first. This course assumes you have transformed a task and taught it.

PD 2 changed the task. PD 3 changes what the teacher does while students work on it.

The principle underneath the whole strategy is one sentence: ask students what they think before showing them what other people think.

It is not an argument against ever explaining. Sometimes telling is exactly right, and a teacher who never tells is withholding rather than teaching. The argument is that telling arrives too early by default, and that most of the thinking available in a lesson is spent before the explanation rather than after it.

The four techniques

  1. Explore before explain — students meet the phenomenon before they meet the account of it
  2. Socratic questioning — the response to an answer is another question
  3. Student voice — students decide something real about how the learning happens
  4. Use dialogue — meaning gets built in conversation, not delivered into silence
Bring the transformed task you taught after PD 2 and the student work from it. Module 2 uses it — you will interrogate your own task using Socratic questioning with colleagues, which is uncomfortable and is the point.

Module 1 — Explore before explain

90 minutes · Outcome: you can design an exploration that makes the later explanation land.

1.1  The default order, and why it is backwards

The standard lesson shape is explain, then practise, then apply. It is efficient and it has one significant cost: a student who has already been given the account has no reason to construct one, and no felt need for the one they are given.

Reversing it — encounter first, account second — is not slower in the way it looks. The explanation, when it comes, arrives to a class that has a question.

1.2  What counts as exploring

Not free discovery, and not guessing. A structured encounter with something the student cannot yet account for.

  • Prediction, committed in writing, before the demonstration.
  • Sorting and classifying, before the categories are named.
  • A hands-on trial where their first idea is allowed to fail.
  • KWHL — what I think I know, what I want to know, how I will find out, what I learned.
  • A concept map made before the topic is taught, and revisited after.
  • A human graph — physically standing on a line to show a position, and having to justify where you stood.
  • The 5E model, if your subject already uses it — engage, explore, explain, elaborate, evaluate.
The commitment is what makes it work. A prediction made silently is easy to revise into whatever happened. A prediction written down and shown to a partner cannot be, and the gap between it and the result is the teaching moment. This is also, not coincidentally, one of the most reliable ways to make a task AI-resistant — a model cannot retro-fit a commitment a student already made.

1.3  Questioning during exploration

Exploration without questioning is activity. The teacher's job while students explore is not to circulate and confirm — it is to ask the question that pushes the thinking one step further.

A small, reusable set does most of the work: What do you notice? · What does that make you think? · What would happen if…? · How do you know? · Is that always true?

The mathematics educator Steve Reinhart's account of his own practice is the useful one here: his students' problem-solving improved when he began talking less and questioning more. The change was not in the tasks but in what he did while students worked on them.

Designing the exploration

A model is genuinely good at this, because generating a phenomenon that a given misconception cannot explain is a pattern-matching problem.

My students believe: [state the misconception in one sentence]. The idea I want them to reach is: [one sentence]. Design an exploration they can do in 15 minutes, before I explain anything, which their current belief cannot account for. It must: - require a written prediction before anything happens - produce a result their belief predicts wrongly - be doable with ordinary classroom equipment Then give me five questions to ask while they work, none of which confirms or denies whether they are right.

That final constraint is the one worth keeping. Most teacher questions during activity are disguised confirmations, and a model will produce those unless you forbid them.

Activity — reverse a lesson

  1. Take a lesson you teach where you currently explain first.
  2. Identify what students believe before the lesson. Be specific — a sentence they would agree with.
  3. Design the encounter their belief cannot account for.
  4. Write the commitment: what exactly do they write down, and when?
  5. Write your five questions, none of which confirm or deny.

Check your understanding

Why does a written, shared prediction matter more than a mental one?

Your colleague says explore-before-explain wastes time when the explanation takes four minutes. What is the strongest response?

Concede the arithmetic — the explanation does take four minutes and the exploration takes fifteen, so the lesson is longer. The response is about what the four minutes buy. Delivered to a class with no question, the explanation is heard and reproduced; the misconception it was meant to overturn survives underneath, and reappears in a term's time, and the four minutes get spent again. Delivered to a class that has just watched its own prediction fail, the same four minutes land on a felt need and are considerably more likely to stick.

It is also worth being honest about the limit: this is not the right shape for every lesson, and using it for content that is simply new rather than counter-intuitive is a genuine waste. Reserve it for the ideas students reliably get wrong. There are usually about five per subject per year.

Module 2 — Socratic questioning

90 minutes · Outcome: you can respond to an answer with a question that goes somewhere.

2.1  What makes a question Socratic

Not that it is hard. Three properties: it is systematic and disciplined, it probes below the surface, and it assesses truth or plausibility rather than checking recall. The defining move is that the questioner responds to whatever answer is given with another question.

Asked "which is heavier, the apple or the lemon?", the Socratic response to any answer is: What are your reasons for thinking that? · Is that always true? · What would change your mind? · How could we find out?

2.2  Six kinds worth having

KindExample
ClarifyingWhat do you mean by that? Can you put it another way?
Probing assumptionsWhat are you taking for granted here? What if the opposite were true?
Probing reasons and evidenceHow do you know? What would count as evidence against it?
Perspectives and alternativesWho would disagree, and what would their best argument be?
ConsequencesIf that is right, what else must be true? What follows?
Questions about the questionWhy does this question matter? Is it the right question to be asking?

These derive from Richard Paul and Linda Elder's work on Socratic questioning, and Michael Pohl's classroom adaptations of it.

The commonest failure is the disguised confirmation. 'Are you sure?' is not a Socratic question — it tells the student they are wrong and asks them to guess again. 'What are your reasons?' asked identically of right and wrong answers is Socratic. If your questioning pattern lets a student infer correctness from your tone, they will stop reasoning and start reading you.

2.3  Safety is not optional here

Sustained questioning feels like interrogation unless the room is set up for it. Students need to know the questioning is not a judgement and not a trap. Agreed protocols do this better than reassurance:

  • Focus on the task and the thinking, not the person.
  • Questions carry positive intent — no ulterior agenda.
  • 'Can I come back to that?' is always available.
  • Share in more than one way.

Domain 2 of the TfEL framework — create safe conditions for rigorous learning — is doing the work here. Rigour and safety are not a trade-off; the rigour is only available because of the safety.

2.4  Teaching students to use them

The larger prize is not you asking better questions. It is students asking them of each other, at which point the thinking continues when you are on the other side of the room. A printed set of question cards on each table, used in threes — one answers, one questions, one observes and swaps — is the fastest route there.

Practising the pattern

One genuinely good use: rehearsing the questioning without a student in front of you.

Play a Year 8 student who believes that heavier objects fall faster. I will ask you questions. Answer exactly as that student would, including being stubborn, changing your mind slowly, and occasionally saying something that sounds right for the wrong reason. Do not correct yourself unless my question genuinely makes you. After ten exchanges, tell me which of my questions did real work and which were disguised confirmations.

The final instruction is the useful half. Most of us discover we ask two good questions and then start steering.

Activity — interrogate your own task

Groups of three. Each person brings the task they taught after PD 2 and the student work.

  1. Person A describes what they did and what happened. Three minutes.
  2. Person B asks only Socratic questions. No advice, no suggestions, no 'have you tried'. Five minutes.
  3. Person C observes and afterwards names which questions did real work.
  4. Rotate. Everyone does all three roles.
  5. Afterwards: which question would you have most liked to be asked, and were you?
Banning advice in step 2 is the whole exercise. Most professional conversation is advice with a question mark on the end, and the difference is immediately obvious once it is forbidden.

Check your understanding

Which is a genuine Socratic question?

Why do agreed protocols matter more than reassurance for Socratic questioning?

Write four Socratic follow-ups to a student who says 'the character was just evil' — none of which signal whether you agree.

Something like: What in the text made you settle on that? · Is there a reading where his actions make sense to him? · What would the author have had to change for you to say something different? · If that is right, what does it mean for the way the ending works?

Each one takes the claim seriously, none discloses your view, and each has a defensible answer. Notice they map onto four of the six kinds — evidence, alternative perspectives, assumptions, consequences. Weak follow-ups here are the ones that leak: 'do you really think so?', 'is he though?', 'what about when he helps his sister?'. That last one is the sneakiest — it is a counter-argument disguised as a question, and the student hears it correctly as being told they are wrong.

Module 3 — Student voice

90 minutes · Outcome: you can give students a real decision without losing control of the learning.

3.1  What student voice is, and is not

It is not asking students what they enjoyed. It is giving them a decision that genuinely changes what happens, at a point where more than one answer is workable.

Not student voiceStudent voice
'Did you like this topic?''Which of these two ways of finding out would give us better evidence?'
Choosing between a poster and a PowerPoint'What would you need to know to answer this, and how will you get it?'
Voting on a film at the end of term'We have three lessons left on this. What do we still not understand?'
A suggestion box'You've seen the criteria. Which of them is doing the least work, and why?'

3.2  Where in a task it fits

At almost any point, and the earlier it sits the more it changes:

  • How to find out — which sources, which method, which measurement.
  • How to show it — the mode of the product, when the mode is not the thing being assessed.
  • What counts as good — students draft the success criteria before seeing yours, then compare.
  • What the question should be — the most demanding version, and the one PD 4 builds on.
Only offer decisions you will honour. A class that has had a choice overridden once will treat the next one as a guessing game about what you wanted. This is the single fastest way to make student voice worse than not doing it.

3.3  The comfort question

Whether a student contributes depends heavily on how they are being asked to contribute, quite independently of whether they have something to say.

Rank these by how comfortable you would be using each to share an idea with a full staff meeting: speaking to the whole room · writing on a shared board · telling one person who reports it · writing anonymously on a slip · drawing it.

Your ranking is probably not the same as the person next to you. Neither is your students'. Offering one mode selects for one kind of student, every time, and the selection has nothing to do with the quality of their thinking.

A model cannot do this one for you

Student voice is the technique with the least AI leverage in the whole programme, and it is worth saying so plainly. A model can generate options; it cannot give a student a real decision, and a decision generated by a model and presented as a choice is a simulation of voice.

The one honest use — pressure-testing your own offer: I am going to let my Year 9 class decide [the decision]. The options they realistically might choose are [list them]. For each option, tell me what I would have to be willing to live with. Then tell me which of these I am likely to override if they choose it — because if I would override it, I should not be offering it.

If the model identifies an option you would quietly veto, remove it from the choice before the lesson rather than after.

Activity — one real decision

  1. Take your transformed task. Identify one decision currently made by you that could be made by students.
  2. Write the exact wording you will use to offer it.
  3. List every choice they might plausibly make. For each, ask honestly: would I let that happen?
  4. Remove any you would override. What remains is your actual offer.
  5. Decide two modes by which students will share their thinking.

Check your understanding

Which is genuine student voice?

You offered a choice and a group chose the option you think is weakest. What now?

Let it run, and plan the reflection. Overriding it teaches the class that the choice was decorative, which costs you every subsequent offer and is not recoverable within a term.

The productive move is to make the weakness visible rather than assert it: ask what would count as evidence that the approach worked, agree it in advance, and hold the comparison afterwards against the group that chose differently. A group that discovers for itself that its method produced weaker evidence has learned something considerably more useful than the method — and if it turns out their approach worked better than you expected, which happens more often than is comfortable, you have learned something too.

The genuine limit is safety and time. If the choice is unsafe or would consume three lessons, it should not have been on the list, and that is a fault in the offer rather than in the students.

Module 4 — Use dialogue

90 minutes · Outcome: you can run a structured dialogue in which students build meaning rather than take turns.

4.1  Discussion and dialogue are not the same

In most classroom discussion, students take turns addressing the teacher. The teacher evaluates each contribution and moves on. It has the shape of dialogue and the structure of assessment, and students read it correctly.

In dialogue, students respond to each other, positions change during the conversation, and the teacher's job is to protect the structure rather than to judge the contributions.

4.2  Community of Inquiry

A democratic, structured dialogue with simple physical rules that do most of the work.

  • Everyone can see everyone. A circle, not rows.
  • A speaking object — whoever holds it talks, and nobody interrupts it.
  • Each participant has a small number of tokens. Speaking costs one. When they are gone, you listen.
  • Contributions build: agree and extend, disagree and say why, or ask.
  • The teacher tracks the thinking and does not evaluate the contributions.
The tokens are the mechanism. They stop the three most confident students from taking eighty per cent of the airtime, and — the part people do not expect — they make those three students think harder, because a contribution now costs something and they start choosing when to spend it.

4.3  Talk that carries the subject

Dialogue is where subject language gets used before it is assessed. A student who has argued a position out loud, using the vocabulary, in front of people who might disagree, writes about it very differently afterwards.

This is the mechanism behind 'from information to understanding' — the fourth Transforming Tasks strategy, which is threaded through these courses rather than taught separately. Information becomes understanding when it is used to do something in front of somebody.

4.4  Sentence stems that raise the level

MoveStem
BuildThat connects to what X said because…
ChallengeI think differently because… What would you say to…?
ClarifyWhen you said X, did you mean…?
EvidenceWhat makes you say that? Where in the text?
ShiftI've changed my mind because…
Give the last one visible status. 'I've changed my mind because…' should be the highest-value move in the room and is usually the rarest. Naming it as the thing you are most pleased to hear changes what a class thinks the conversation is for.

Preparing a dialogue, not running one

Nothing about this technique needs a model in the room. Where one helps is in the twenty minutes before the lesson.

I want a 20-minute Community of Inquiry with Year 7 on [the idea]. My students currently believe [the misconception]. Give me: - one stimulus question with no settled answer that a 12-year-old can have a view on within ten seconds - three positions students are likely to take, including the one built on the misconception - for each, the question I could ask that would test it without me taking a side - two sentence stems for students who have not spoken

Then put the model away. A dialogue with a model open on the desk is not a dialogue.

Activity — run one

In your table group, using the 'From tell to ask' examples you have collected.

  1. Sit so everyone can see everyone. Five tokens each and one speaking object.
  2. Twelve minutes on: which of these four techniques would be hardest to use in my subject, and what does that tell us?
  3. One person tracks the thinking rather than participating — where did positions move?
  4. Afterwards: who spoke least, and was it because they had least to say?

Check your understanding

What most reliably distinguishes dialogue from classroom discussion?

Three students dominate every discussion in your class. Why might tokens work better than 'let someone else speak'?

Because the instruction addresses the symptom and puts the teacher in the position of policing it every time, which is both exhausting and reads as a rebuke to students who are doing what the room has always rewarded. Tokens change the structure instead: the limit is visible, applies to everyone equally, and is enforced by the rule rather than by you.

The less obvious effect is on the three. A finite budget makes them selective, and selection requires judgement about which of their thoughts is worth spending on — so their contributions typically get better as well as fewer. And the quiet students are not being asked to overcome anything; the airtime simply exists, which is usually all that was missing.
