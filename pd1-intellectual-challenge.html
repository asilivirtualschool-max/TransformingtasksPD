Intellectual Challenge

What makes a task worth setting — the foundation for PD 2, 3 and 4
Four modules · 6 hours · self-paced · portfolio-assessed

Before you start

Read this, then work through the modules in order. PD 1 is the foundation — PD 2, 3 and 4 all assume it.

This course is not about a new initiative and it does not ask you to replace what you already do. It asks one question, about work you are already setting: what thinking does this task actually demand of a student?

Not what the topic is. Not what the verb in the instruction says. What a student has to do in their head to complete it — and whether they could complete it without doing much at all.

Who it is for

  • Classroom teachers in any subject, primary or secondary.
  • Heads of department and curriculum leads auditing schemes of work.
  • Anyone running professional learning on task design or AI use.

No prior knowledge is assumed and no AI experience is needed. Bring one task you will set in the next fortnight. You will work on it throughout, and you will leave with it transformed.

Module 1 — Level and kind of thinking

90 minutes · Outcome: you can say what thinking a task demands, and tell that apart from what the task is about.

1.1  Two questions that look the same and are not

Try these in pairs. Do the first, then the second, and notice what changed.

Question AQuestion B
EnglishList the characters in the film you saw most recently.Which characters in that film are most and least like you, and why?
ScienceWrite down what you know about what causes earthquakes.What would you need to know in order to predict where the next one will happen?
HistoryPut the last ten prime ministers in chronological order.Put them in order of how influential they were.
MathsWork out how many leap years remain this century.Who would need to know that, and what would they do with it?

The topic is identical in every row. The subject knowledge required is nearly identical. What changes is what the student has to do with it.

The B questions are subjective, or depend on the student's perspective, or require a definition the student has to supply themselves. They have more than one defensible answer. They require the student to construct something rather than retrieve it. And — this is the part that matters most for the rest of these four courses — the B questions cannot be completed without the student thinking.

1.2  Level and kind

When we talk about the challenge in a task we are talking about the student thinking the task demands. There are two dimensions of it, and they are not the same thing.

Level

How demanding the thinking is. Recalling a definition sits low; justifying a choice between two defensible positions sits high.

Kind

Which thinking processes are involved — recalling, remembering, applying, analysing, evaluating, justifying, creating. Two tasks can sit at a similar level and demand entirely different kinds of thinking.

Students need a range of both. This is not an argument that high-level thinking is good and low-level thinking is bad. A student who cannot recall fluently has nothing to think with. The argument is that most task banks are unbalanced in one direction, and nobody planned it that way.

1.3  The verb is not the evidence

The commonest mistake in analysing a task is to look for the verb. "Evaluate" appears in the instruction, therefore the task demands evaluation. It very often does not.

Consider: "Evaluate the causes of the First World War using the table on page 42." If page 42 contains a ranked table of causes, the student is copying, not evaluating. The verb says one thing; the thinking demanded is retrieval.

The reverse also happens. "What do you notice?" contains no impressive verb at all and can demand very high-level thinking, because the student has to decide what is worth noticing.

The working test. Ask: could a student complete this successfully without doing the thinking I intended? If yes, the task measures compliance. Ask it of the task, not of the instruction.

Where AI comes in

A model will happily generate a task with an impressive verb in it. It will not, unprompted, generate a task that requires thinking, because it was asked for a task on a topic. You have to ask for the thinking. Compare what you get from these two briefs:

Weak: Write a Year 8 history task on the causes of the First World War. Strong: Write a Year 8 history task on the causes of the First World War. The task must have more than one defensible answer. Students must supply their own criterion for "most important" rather than being given one. A student who copies from a textbook table must not be able to complete it. Do not use the word "evaluate" in the instruction.

The second brief is longer than the task it produces. That is normal, and it is the actual skill this course teaches — the model does the writing, you do the specifying.

Activity — two versions

  1. Take the task you brought. Write down, in one sentence, the thinking you intend a student to do.
  2. Now write down what a student could actually do to complete it successfully. Be honest — include the shortcut.
  3. If those two sentences are different, you have found the gap this whole programme works on.

Check your understanding

A task instruction begins 'Analyse the poem's use of imagery' and provides a completed table of the poem's images with their effects. What thinking does the task demand?

Which is the better description of 'kind' of thinking, as distinct from 'level'?

Take a task you set last week. Write the one sentence describing what a student could do to complete it without doing the thinking you intended.

A strong answer is uncomfortable and specific. Something like: 'They could find the three causes listed in the box on the worksheet and copy them into the three lines provided, in the order they appear.' If you cannot write such a sentence, either the task is genuinely well designed — which happens — or you are describing what you hoped rather than what is possible. The test of a good answer here is whether a slightly disengaged, capable student would recognise it as exactly what they do.

Module 2 — Fluency and fluency plus

90 minutes · Outcome: you can name which kind of thinking a task builds, and explain why every student needs both.

2.1  The two kinds

Professor Martin Westwell's distinction, drawn from national assessment data, is the most useful shorthand in this whole area.

Fluency

Recalling information, or applying a procedure that has already been learned. A student can be equipped for this through explicit teaching and practice, and it works.

Fluency plus

Problem solving, reasoning, justifying, connecting to prior understanding. A student cannot be equipped for this through explicit teaching and practice alone, because the situation is by definition one they have not been shown.

2.2  The pattern in the data

In the South Australian analysis that generated this framework, roughly a third of national test questions were fluency questions, and students did well on them. The remaining two thirds required problem solving and reasoning, and that is where performance dropped.

The conclusion drawn was not that schools teach badly. It was narrower and more useful: we already have effective strategies for building fluency, and comparatively few for building fluency plus. The greatest available improvement is in the two thirds, because that is where the gap between what students can do and what is asked of them is widest.

Neither is being prioritised over the other. This is the single most misread message in the framework. A student who cannot recall place value fluently cannot reason about decimals. Fluency is the material that reasoning works on. The argument is about balance, not replacement.

2.3  What this means for a task bank

Audit any scheme of work and you will typically find the fluency tasks are numerous, well sequenced and carefully differentiated, and the fluency plus tasks are the last question on the sheet, marked "extension", and reached by about four students.

That arrangement makes a specific and damaging claim: that reasoning is what you do after you have finished the real work, and that it is for the students who are quick. Both halves of that are wrong.

2.4  All students, not the top group

Historically, students who struggle in a subject have been given more of what they can already do and less of what they cannot. The reasoning is compassionate and the effect is the opposite of what is intended: the students who most need practice at reasoning get the least of it, and the gap compounds every year.

The key message of this module. ALL students need to engage in both fluency and fluency plus tasks. The differentiation happens in the scaffolding and the support, never in whether the thinking is required. That principle is what the next three courses are techniques for.

The AI trap, stated plainly

Ask a model for practice questions on a topic and you will get fluency questions. Excellent ones, in volume, correctly levelled, instantly. This is genuinely useful and it is also the reason unmanaged AI use tilts a task bank further toward fluency than it already was.

Ask for both, explicitly and in a ratio: Produce six questions on converting improper fractions. Two should be fluency: the procedure, practised. Four should be fluency plus: the student must reason about why the procedure works, or apply it in a situation where the procedure alone is not enough, or decide whether it applies at all. For each of the four, tell me what a student would have to understand in order to answer it.

That last line is the quality check. If the model cannot say what understanding the question requires, it has produced a fluency question wearing a longer sentence.

Activity — audit the balance

  1. Take one unit from your scheme of work. Count the tasks.
  2. Sort every task into fluency or fluency plus. Where you hesitate, ask whether a student could do it by following a taught procedure.
  3. Write the ratio down.
  4. Now look at which tasks the students who find your subject hardest actually did. Sort those too.
  5. Compare the two ratios. This second number is the one worth taking to a department meeting.

Check your understanding

Why can't students be equipped for 'fluency plus' questions through explicit teaching and practice alone?

A department decides to give its lowest-attaining set extra procedural practice and reserve open problem-solving tasks for the top set. What is the strongest objection?

Your school has bought an AI tool that generates differentiated worksheets on any topic in seconds. What is the risk to the balance you audited above, and what would you do about it?

The risk is that generation is cheapest for exactly the task type you already have too much of. A model produces graded procedural practice beautifully, so the marginal cost of adding fluency tasks falls to almost nothing while the cost of designing a good fluency plus task does not fall at all — it still requires a teacher to decide what thinking is wanted. Left alone, the ratio worsens and everyone feels more productive. The response is not to restrict the tool: it is to make the ratio an explicit part of the brief, and to review the balance at unit level rather than task level, because the drift is invisible one worksheet at a time.

Module 3 — Reading the challenge in a task

90 minutes · Outcome: you can locate a task on a thinking continuum, and transform one while keeping its intended learning.

3.1  The continuum

There are several established ways of describing the level and kind of thinking in a task. The Transforming Tasks guide draws on three of them together — Bloom's revised taxonomy, Webb's Depth of Knowledge and the SOLO taxonomy — because each catches something the others miss.

FrameworkWhat it is good atWhat it misses
Bloom (revised)Naming the kind of cognitive process — remember, understand, apply, analyse, evaluate, createIt is routinely read as a hierarchy of task difficulty, which it is not. It also invites the verb trap.
Webb's Depth of KnowledgeDescribing how deeply a student must engage — recall, skill/concept, strategic thinking, extended thinkingLess helpful for naming which process is involved.
SOLODescribing the structure of a response — one idea, several ideas, ideas related, ideas extendedFocused on the answer produced rather than the task set.
Use these to analyse the student thinking the task requires — not to check which words appear in the instruction. This is the point at which most task audits go wrong, and it is worth saying out loud in a department meeting before anyone starts.

3.2  Critical and creative thinking

Alongside the level of thinking sits a second question: is the task asking students to think critically — analysing, evaluating, reasoning about what is there — or creatively — generating possibilities, imagining alternatives, taking an intellectual risk?

Most task banks are much stronger on the critical side. Creative thinking is often assumed to belong to the arts, and it does not: designing a fair test, generating a conjecture, and proposing an alternative interpretation are all creative acts inside analytical subjects.

3.3  The before-and-after method

The method underneath all four of these courses is simple and it does not require you to redesign your curriculum.

  1. Take a task you already set. This is the before.
  2. Name its intended learning. Not the topic — what you want students to end up understanding.
  3. Analyse the thinking it currently demands.
  4. Transform it so that it demands more, or a different kind, while keeping the same intended learning.
  5. Compare the two.
The constraint in step 4 is the whole discipline. It is easy to make a task more demanding by making it about something else, or by adding a research component, or by making it longer. None of those is a transformation. The intended learning has to survive intact, or you have swapped a task rather than improved one.

3.4  A worked comparison

BeforeAfter
TaskLabel the parts of a flower on the diagram provided.Here are three flowers. One is pollinated by insects, one by wind, one by birds. Which is which, and what evidence are you using?
Intended learningFlower structure and the function of each part.Unchanged.
Thinking demandedRecall and match. Retrieval from a labelled reference.Analysis, inference from structure to function, and justification from evidence.
Could a student succeed without it?Yes — by copying from the textbook diagram.No — the answer is not written anywhere and the reasoning is the answer.
Entry pointOne. You know the labels or you do not.Several. A student can start from colour, size, shape or scent and still be reasoning.

Using a model for the before-and-after

This is the most immediately useful AI move in the whole programme, and it is a comparison rather than a generation. Give the model your existing task and ask it to analyse before it rewrites.

Here is a task I currently set: [paste it]. The intended learning is: [one sentence]. First, tell me what thinking a student actually has to do to complete it successfully — including any shortcut that would work. Do not rewrite it yet. Then produce three transformed versions that demand more or different thinking while keeping the same intended learning. For each, state what changed and what a student can no longer get away with.

Insisting on the analysis before the rewrite is what stops the model from simply producing a longer, more elaborate task. And ask for three: the first is usually predictable, and the third is often the interesting one.

Activity — your before and after

  1. Write your before task exactly as students see it.
  2. Write its intended learning in one sentence.
  3. Analyse the current thinking: level, kind, and the shortcut.
  4. Transform it. Work alone for five minutes first, then with the model, then decide which version you prefer and why.
  5. Write down the general rule your transformation followed. You will need it — those rules are what PD 2, 3 and 4 are made of.

Check your understanding

A teacher makes a task harder by requiring students to research two extra historical figures before answering. Is this a transformation?

Rewrite this so it demands reasoning rather than retrieval, keeping the intended learning: 'List three causes of the Industrial Revolution.'

Something in the shape of: 'Here are three causes of the Industrial Revolution. Remove the one the Revolution could most plausibly have happened without, and defend the removal.' The intended learning — knowing and understanding the causes — is unchanged and is in fact tested harder, because a student who has merely memorised a list cannot decide which is dispensable. Note what the rewrite did: it supplied the content and asked for a judgement about it, rather than asking for the content. That is one of the most portable moves in the whole programme, and you will meet it again in PD 2 as 'different perspectives'.

Module 4 — Conditions for challenge

90 minutes · Outcome: you can raise demand without losing the students who find your subject hardest.

4.1  Challenge needs safety

A task that demands real thinking also demands that a student risk being wrong in public. Whether they will take that risk is not a property of the task. It is a property of the room.

The Teaching for Effective Learning framework organises this into three big ideas:

Create safe conditions for rigorous learning

Students will take intellectual risks when being wrong is survivable and useful rather than exposing.

Develop expert learners

Students who can monitor their own thinking, and who know that struggle is part of learning rather than evidence against them.

Personalise and connect learning

Work that connects to what the student already knows and cares about, and which has more than one way in.

Element 2.4 of that framework — challenge students to achieve high standards with appropriate support — is the one this programme sits inside. Both halves of it matter, and dropping either produces a recognisable failure: challenge without support is abandonment, support without challenge is busywork.

4.2  Multiple entry and exit points

The most practical single move in this module. A task with one entry point sorts the class into those who can start and those who cannot. A task with several allows every student to begin within a minute, and allows the strongest not to finish.

One entry pointSeveral
TaskCalculate the mean of this data set.Here is a data set. What can you say about it? What would you want to know?
A student who is behindCannot start without the procedure.Can start by describing what they notice.
A student who is aheadFinishes in ninety seconds, then waits.Can pursue spread, outliers, or whether the mean is the right measure at all.
What the teacher learnsWhether they can execute the procedure.Where each student's thinking currently reaches.

4.3  Scaffolding that does not remove the thinking

The instinct when a student struggles with a demanding task is to make the task easier. Almost always this means removing the part that required thinking, and what remains is a procedure.

The alternative is to change the support without changing the demand.

Instead ofTry
Giving the stepsGiving the first step only, or giving the steps out of order
Giving a worked example to copyGiving a worked example with one error in it
Reducing the number of variablesKeeping the variables and supplying the vocabulary
Telling them the answer formShowing two finished answers of different quality and asking which is better
Moving them to an easier taskKeeping the task and giving them longer, or a partner

4.4  Mindset, briefly and accurately

Carol Dweck's distinction between fixed and growth mindsets matters here for one specific reason. A student who believes ability is fixed reads struggle as evidence that they lack ability, and the rational response to that belief is to avoid tasks that produce struggle — which is precisely the tasks this programme is asking you to set more of.

The teaching consequence is narrow and worth doing: say out loud that the task is hard, that being stuck is expected, and that it will resolve. That is not lowering expectations. It is removing the inference that being stuck means being incapable.

It is not only students who struggle who need this. Students with consistently high results are often the most reluctant to persevere with a genuinely open task, because they have more to lose from visible failure and less practice at it.

Where AI helps with conditions, and where it does not

AI is good at the support and useless at the safety. It can produce a glossary, a sentence-stem bank, a first-step-only scaffold, or the same task written at three reading levels — in seconds, which is what makes multiple entry points practical rather than aspirational.

Take this task and produce three supports that do NOT reduce the thinking required: 1. a vocabulary and sentence-stem sheet 2. a version with the first step given and the rest removed 3. a worked example containing exactly one error of reasoning, with the error not flagged Do not simplify the task itself in any version.

What it cannot do is make a student willing to be wrong in front of their class. That is built by what you do when the first student gets it wrong, and no tool has any part in it.

Activity — one task, three supports

Take your transformed task from Module 3.

  1. Identify the student in your class most likely to be unable to start it.
  2. Design a support that lets them start without removing the thinking.
  3. Identify the student most likely to finish in two minutes. What is the ceiling that stops them stopping?
  4. Write the sentence you will say to the class before setting it, naming the difficulty honestly.

Check your understanding

Which is a scaffold that preserves the thinking?

Why does telling a class 'this is genuinely hard and being stuck is expected' help?

A colleague says the open version of your task will 'leave the weak students behind'. Give the strongest version of their concern, and then your response.

The strongest version of their concern is real and should not be dismissed: an open task with a single implicit entry point can leave a student staring at a page with no way in, and fifteen minutes of visible failure teaches something worse than a completed worksheet would have. That is a genuine risk and it is what badly designed open tasks actually do.

The response is that the answer to it is entry points and support, not a lower demand. Multiple entry points mean the student starts within a minute at whatever level they can. Supports that supply vocabulary, a first step, or a flawed example to critique give them something to work on without doing the thinking for them. And the alternative — a steady diet of procedural work — reliably produces students who cannot reason, which is a certainty rather than a risk. The colleague is right about the failure mode and wrong about the remedy.
