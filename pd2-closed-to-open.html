The four techniques in this course

  1. Different perspectives — the answer changes depending on where you stand
  2. Many solutions — more than one answer is defensible
  3. Many pathways — more than one route to the answer
  4. Many entry points — more than one place to start

They are not mutually exclusive and the best transformations usually use two.

Bring the task you taught after PD 1, and what happened when you taught it — including what went wrong. Module 1 opens with it.

Module 1 — Closed, open, or it depends

90 minutes · Outcome: you can tell an open question from a closed one, and explain why the distinction is less tidy than it looks.

1.1  Sort these

For each, decide: closed, open, or it depends. Argue about the ones you disagree on — the argument is the learning, and 'it depends' is the most instructive answer in the set.

QuestionVerdictWhy
What is the capital of France?ClosedOne answer, verifiable, no reasoning required.
Why is Paris the capital of France?DependsOpen if students must construct a historical argument. Closed if the textbook gives the reason and they copy it.
What is 7 × 8?ClosedOne answer.
How many ways can you make 56?OpenUnbounded, and the constraint students set themselves determines the difficulty.
What happened in the story?DependsUsually closed in practice — retelling. Open if 'what happened' is genuinely contested in the text.
Was the character right to do that?OpenNo single defensible answer; requires a criterion the student supplies.
What do you notice?OpenDemands the student decide what is worth noticing, which is a high-level judgement disguised as an easy question.

1.2  What actually makes a question open

Not the wording. Three structural properties, and a question needs at least one:

  • More than one defensible answer. Not more than one possible answer — more than one that can be argued for.
  • The student supplies a criterion. "Most important", "best", "most like you" — the word is only meaningful once the student decides what it means.
  • The reasoning is the answer. The response cannot be graded without reading the justification.
Open does not mean vague. 'Write about the war' is not open, it is unspecified. An open task is tightly specified about the thinking and loose about the answer. The commonest failed transformation is a task that got vaguer instead of more open, and students find those harder to start and less useful to do.

1.3  Constraints make open tasks better

Counter-intuitively, adding a constraint usually raises the challenge in an open task rather than lowering it.

"How many ways can you make 56?" is open and quickly becomes a list. "How many ways can you make 56 using exactly three numbers and two different operations?" is more open in the sense that matters — it requires strategy, forces the student to notice structure, and produces reasoning rather than enumeration.

Getting a model to open a question

Models default to closed. They are optimised to be helpful and correct, and a closed question is easier to be correct about. You have to specify the properties above.

Rewrite this question so it is open: [paste your question] Open means: more than one defensible answer, the student must supply the criterion for judging, and the reasoning is the answer. Add a constraint that makes it harder rather than vaguer. Then tell me what a student would have to decide for themselves that they did not have to decide before.

That last sentence is the check. If the model cannot name what the student now has to decide, the question is not open — it is just longer.

Activity — bring back your PD 1 task

  1. Share what happened when you taught your transformed task. Two minutes: what you changed, what students did, what surprised you.
  2. Sort your own current questions from one unit into closed, open and depends.
  3. Take the three you were least sure about and say why.

Check your understanding

Which is the best reason 'What do you notice?' can be a demanding question?

A teacher changes 'Describe the water cycle' to 'Write about water'. What has gone wrong?

Take a closed question you set regularly. Add a constraint that makes it more demanding rather than vaguer.

A strong answer keeps the original content and adds something the student must now work around. 'Find the area of this rectangle' becomes 'Find the area of this rectangle without using the formula, and explain why your method works'. Or: 'Give three causes of the war' becomes 'Give three causes, only one of which could have been noticed by someone alive at the time'. In both cases the content is unchanged, the answer is no longer retrievable, and the constraint is what forces the reasoning. Weak answers add words rather than constraints — 'in detail', 'fully', 'in your own words' — none of which changes what a student has to think.

Module 2 — The four techniques

90 minutes · Outcome: you can name and apply all four, and say which suits a given task.

2.1  Different perspectives

Our thinking can move beyond one point of view. Ask how someone else would answer, and the question stops having one answer.

Examples. "How would a farmer answer this, and how would a hydrologist?" · "Rewrite this account as the person on the other side of it." · "Which of these two historians would find your evidence more convincing?"

2.2  Many solutions

The task has an open-ended set of valid answers, and the thinking is stretched by the constraints you add rather than by the size of the set.

Examples. "How many ways can you solve this that meet these criteria?" · "Design three different fair tests for this question, and say which you would trust most." · "Find a counter-example."

2.3  Many pathways

One answer, many legitimate routes to it — and the route becomes the object of discussion.

Examples. "Solve it in more than one way." · "Here are two students' methods, both correct. Which generalises better?" · "What is the most efficient route, and how do you know?"

2.4  Many entry points

More than one place to begin, so every student starts within a minute and nobody is waiting for permission.

Examples. "Here is the data — what can you say about it?" · "Start wherever you can and tell me where you started." · Give the answer and ask for the question.

TechniqueBest whenWatch for
Different perspectivesThe content has stakeholders, or the judgement depends on valuesPerspectives that are decorative — 'imagine you are a Roman' with nothing hanging on it
Many solutionsThere is a solution space to explore, and constraints to imposeTurning into enumeration. Add a constraint that forces strategy
Many pathwaysThe method matters as much as the answerStudents producing one method and a paraphrase of it
Many entry pointsThe class range is wide, or students routinely fail to startA ceiling that arrives too soon, leaving the quickest with nothing
The strongest transformations usually combine two — most often many entry points with one of the others, because entry points solve access and the other three supply the demand.

A model is good at three of these and poor at one

Different perspectives, many solutions and many pathways are pattern-shaped moves and a model does them well. Many entry points is the one it does badly, because judging where your students can start requires knowing them.

Transform this task using the 'many entry points' technique: [paste task] My class: [describe the range honestly — what the students who find this hardest can already do, and what the quickest will do in the first two minutes]. Give me three different opening moves a student could make, from the least to the most sophisticated, all of which are legitimate starts on this task. Then tell me what stops the quickest student finishing in ninety seconds.

The description of your class is what does the work here. Without it you will get generic scaffolding.

Activity — four corners

Four techniques, four rounds. Work with a partner.

  1. Take one 'before' task. Transform it using different perspectives. Five minutes.
  2. Same task, using many solutions. Five minutes.
  3. Same task, many pathways.
  4. Same task, many entry points.
  5. Compare the four. Which is strongest for this particular task, and why? That judgement is the skill.

Check your understanding

'Solve this equation in two different ways and say which method you would teach someone else.' Which technique is this?

Why is 'many entry points' the technique a model handles worst?

Take 'Explain the causes of coastal erosion.' Transform it twice — once with different perspectives, once with many entry points.

Different perspectives: 'A homeowner, a council engineer and an ecologist are asked whether the sea wall should be rebuilt. Write the strongest version of each answer, then say which you find most convincing and what would change your mind.' The content is unchanged; the student now has to hold three positions and adjudicate.

Many entry points: 'Here are four photographs of the same stretch of coast taken twenty years apart, and a table of storm frequency. What can you say? What would you want to know next?' A student can start from what they can see, from the numbers, or from a question — all three are legitimate openings — and there is no ceiling, because 'what would you want to know next' keeps going.

Note that the first raises demand and the second widens access. Doing both to the same task is usually the best version.

Module 3 — Transform your own

90 minutes · Outcome: a task you will teach next week, transformed, with the supports built.

3.1  The discipline, restated

Keep the intended learning. Change what the student has to do with it. If the transformed task teaches something different, you have swapped tasks rather than transformed one.

3.2  Worked transformation

BeforeAfter
TaskUsing the table, state which of the four materials is the best insulator.You have four materials and one hour. Decide which is the best insulator, and be ready to defend both your answer and your method.
Intended learningThermal insulation; interpreting comparative data.Unchanged.
Techniques used—Many pathways (how to test), many entry points (any material, any method), and 'best' is a criterion the student must define.
Shortcut closedReading one row of a table.There is no table. The data does not exist until they make it.
What the teacher now seesWhether they can read a table.How they control variables, what they count as 'best', and whether they notice the hour is a constraint on method.

3.3  The three checks before you set it

  1. Could a student succeed without doing the thinking? If yes, close the shortcut.
  2. Can every student start within ninety seconds? If no, add an entry point — not a simpler task.
  3. Can the quickest student exhaust it in five minutes? If yes, you have no ceiling. Add a constraint or a 'what would you want to know next'.
Do these three in order and do not skip the second. Most failed open tasks fail at access, not at demand — and the teacher concludes the approach does not work when what failed was the entry.

The full brief, end to end

This is the prompt worth saving. It does the analysis, the transformation and the supports in one pass, and it forces the model to declare what it changed.

TASK I CURRENTLY SET: [paste exactly as students see it] INTENDED LEARNING (must not change): [one sentence] MY CLASS: [the range, honestly — what the students who find this hardest can already do, and what the quickest will do in two minutes] Do this in four steps and label them: 1. What thinking does the current task demand, including the shortcut a disengaged but capable student would take? 2. Transform it using two of: different perspectives, many solutions, many pathways, many entry points. Name which two. 3. Give three supports that do not reduce the thinking. 4. State what a student can no longer get away with, and what stops the quickest finishing in ninety seconds.

Then reject the first version. It is usually competent and predictable. Ask for one that is 'less obvious, and riskier' and compare.

Activity — have a go

  1. Choose a task you will genuinely teach in the next two weeks. Not a hypothetical one.
  2. Transform it, alone, for ten minutes. No model.
  3. Now run the full brief above and compare.
  4. Build the final version — usually parts of both.
  5. Run the three checks. Fix whichever fails.
  6. Write down what you will look for in students' work that would tell you the transformation worked.
Do it in that order. Ten minutes alone first is not a ritual — it is what makes you able to judge the model's version. Going to the model first produces a good task you cannot critique.

Check your understanding

Your open task produced fifteen minutes of silence and very little writing. What is the most likely cause?

What would you look for in students' work that would tell you a transformation actually worked?

Weak answers describe engagement — 'they were more involved', 'the room was buzzing'. Those are worth noticing and prove nothing, because a novel task produces energy whatever its demand.

Strong answers name something visible in the work that could not have appeared before. Different students reaching different defensible conclusions. A justification that uses a criterion the student chose and named. Two methods on one page. A student who started somewhere unexpected. A wrong answer that is wrong for an interesting reason rather than a careless one. The general test: could this piece of work have been produced by the 'before' task? If yes, the transformation changed the wrapper.

Module 4 — Safe conditions, and what to do when it goes wrong

90 minutes · Outcome: you can run an open task in a real room, including the parts that do not go to plan.

4.1  The first time is the hard time

A class used to closed tasks will treat an open one as a closed task whose answer you are withholding. Expect: "but what do you actually want?", students waiting to be told the method, and work that stops the moment one plausible answer appears.

This is not resistance. It is a reasonable inference from several years of evidence about how your lessons work. It changes when the evidence changes, and that takes more than one lesson.

4.2  Four things to say

WhenSay
Setting it'There is more than one good answer to this. I am interested in which one you can defend.'
First hand goes up asking what you want'I want your answer and your reason. If two of you disagree and both can defend it, that is a good outcome, not a problem.'
A student is stuck at the start'Tell me one thing you notice.' Then build from it. Never supply the first step if you can extract it.
Someone finishes fast'Convince me someone could reasonably disagree with you.'

4.3  The conditions this needs

An open task asks students to commit to a position in front of other people. Whether they will do that depends on what happened the last time somebody in that room was wrong.

Group protocols, agreed with the class rather than announced at it, do more work here than any task design:

  • Focus on the task and the thinking, not the person.
  • Questions are asked with positive intent — no ulterior agenda.
  • 'Come back to me' is always available.
  • More than one way to share: talk, write, draw, show a partner.
Multiple ways to share is not a courtesy. Ranking how comfortable you would be presenting to a whole staff meeting versus writing something on a slip of paper is a quick way to notice that the mode of sharing changes who participates, entirely independently of who has something to say.

4.4  Assessing open work without losing your evenings

The standard objection to open tasks is marking load, and it is a fair one — twenty-eight different answers cannot be marked against a mark scheme.

They can be sorted. Read the set once and sort into four piles rather than marking each: reasoned and defensible; an answer with the reasoning missing; a criterion borrowed from you rather than chosen; has not engaged with the openness at all — treated it as closed. The pile sizes are your next lesson. This takes about ten minutes for a class set and tells you more than ticks would.

What not to put into a model

One rule, and it is not negotiable: no student names, no identifiable student work, no photographs of exit tickets or books. Aggregate counts and anonymised quotations only.

Safe: I set this open task [paste task]. Of 28 students, 6 gave a reasoned defensible answer, 11 gave an answer with no reasoning, 7 used my criterion rather than choosing one, and 4 treated it as a closed question. What does that distribution suggest, and what should the next lesson do? Not safe: any photograph of student work, any name, any detail that would identify a child.

This is also a licence and safeguarding matter in most schools, not only good practice. Check your own school's AI policy before the first lesson, not after it.

Activity — plan the first ten minutes

For the task you built in Module 3:

  1. Write the exact words you will use to set it.
  2. Write your response to 'but what do you actually want?'
  3. Name the student most likely to sit and wait, and decide what you will say to them.
  4. Decide how students will share — and give at least two modes.
  5. Decide what the four piles will look like for this task specifically.

Check your understanding

A class repeatedly asks 'what do you actually want?' during an open task. What does this most likely indicate?

Your head of department says open tasks are unmarkable at scale. Give the strongest version of the objection and your answer.

The strongest version: with five classes of thirty and a two-week turnaround, work that has to be read and judged individually rather than checked against a mark scheme is genuinely unsustainable, and a strategy that only works when you have time is not a strategy — it is a demonstration lesson.

The answer is that open work should be sorted rather than marked. Four piles — reasoned, answer without reasoning, borrowed criterion, treated it as closed — takes ten minutes for a class set, produces the next lesson directly, and is more informative than a set of ticks. Individual written feedback is reserved for the pieces where it will change something. And it is worth conceding the real limit honestly: you cannot set an open task every lesson and read it properly, so the question is which two per unit carry the reasoning, not how to make all of them open.
