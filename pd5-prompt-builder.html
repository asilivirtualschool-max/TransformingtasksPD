Before you start

PD 1 to 4 first if you can. This course assumes you can already say what thinking a task demands — because that is what you will be asking a model to build.

PD 2, 3 and 4 taught what to change about a task. This one is narrower and more practical: how to ask for it.

Most teachers' prompts fail the same way. They describe the topic and the format and say nothing about the thinking, so the model returns something fluent, correctly levelled and completable without understanding — exactly the task PD 1 taught you to reject.

The architecture has four stages. Each fixes a different failure.

StageNameFrameworkWhat it fixes
1The FuelMAP — Memory, Assets, ActionsThe model does not know your class, your unit or your constraints, so it invents an average one.
2The EngineAIM — Actor, Input, MissionVague about who is answering, what they work from, and what done looks like.
3The CrucibleIterate and verifyThe first draft gets accepted. It is rarely the best available and often wrong in ways you would not check.
4The PolishOCEAN — Original, Concrete, Evident, Assertive, NarrativeOutput that is correct and reads like every other generated resource.

The workspace

This course has a tool attached. the Prompt Builder walks the four stages, assembles the prompt as you type, and has the four Transforming Tasks differentiation modes and twenty formative check-ins wired into it. Open it in a second tab and build as you read.

Everything saves in this browser. Course and builder both use localStorage only — no account, no server, nothing uploaded. The builder's Copy button puts the finished prompt on your clipboard for ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini.

What you will produce

  • Three full prompts through all four stages, for tasks you are actually setting.
  • A before-and-after: the same request written naively and written through the architecture, with both outputs.
  • An account of what you rejected from the model and why.

Module 1 — The Fuel (MAP)

90 minutes · Outcome: you can ground a request so the model stops writing for an imaginary average class.

1.1  Generic output is an input problem

Ask for "a Year 8 worksheet on ratio" and you get one — for a class that does not exist, in a school that does not exist, with no constraints. The output is generic because the input was.

MAP supplies three things the model cannot guess.

1.2  Memory — the situation

Who the class is, where you are in the unit, how long the lesson is, what they did last week, what they got wrong.

Weak

"Year 8 science."

Strong

"Grade 8 science, mid-unit on ecosystems, 50-minute periods. Last week they could name the parts of a food chain but could not say what happens to the energy. Two students are new to English."

1.3  Assets — what it works from

Your scheme of work, the standard, last week's task, the rubric you already use.

This is the highest-leverage line in most prompts and the one most often missing. A model working from your materials produces something that fits your unit. A model working from nothing produces something that fits the average of the internet.

1.4  Actions — what it does first

Search, read the attachment, compute, or nothing. "None needed" is a legitimate answer and it stops the model padding.

The test for a good MAP. Could a supply teacher who had never met your class produce the right task from what you have written? If not, neither can the model.

Activity — fuel one real request

  1. Open the Prompt Builder and pick the preset closest to what you are setting this week, or start blank.
  2. Write Memory as if briefing a colleague covering your class tomorrow. Four sentences, not four words.
  3. List your Assets honestly, including the ones you have not attached yet. Go and attach them.
  4. Decide Actions. If the answer is none, write none.
  5. Watch the prompt card assemble. It should already be longer than what you would normally have sent.

Check your understanding

Which element of MAP is highest-leverage for most teacher prompts?

Write the Memory line for a lesson you teach this week, as if briefing a colleague covering your class.

A strong answer contains the year group and subject, where you are in the unit, the lesson length, and — the part almost everyone omits — what students got wrong last time. That last detail turns a generic task into a targeted one, and it is the direct link back to PD 1: the misconception is what the task exists to overturn, so the model needs to know it.

A weak answer is a label: 'Year 9 history, Weimar.' That tells the model the topic, which it could have inferred from your Mission, and nothing about the class.

Module 2 — The Engine (AIM)

90 minutes · Outcome: a command with a defined voice, defined inputs and a checkable finish line.

2.1  Actor

Who is answering — specific enough to change the output.

Weak

"You are a teacher."

Strong

"You are a veteran Grade 8 science teacher and instructional designer, known for hands-on lessons that surface misconceptions early."

The second has a bias you can predict. That is the point: you are choosing what kind of wrong you would rather be.

2.2  Input

What the model is working from for this request. Assets is what exists; Input is what you are pointing at now.

2.3  Mission

The finish line, stated so completion is checkable. A Mission that cannot be failed cannot be judged.

Weak MissionStrong Mission
Make a lesson on food webs.Design a 50-minute lesson on food webs with a hook, one hands-on activity and a five-question exit ticket whose wrong options are real misconceptions.
Write a rubric.Write a four-point rubric for persuasive essay — claim, evidence, organisation, conventions — in language a Year 6 student can self-assess with.
Differentiate this.Produce three versions at the same learning goal: one with the first step given, one with a worked example containing an error, one with no scaffold and a harder ceiling.
The completion test. Read your Mission and ask whether you could tell in ten seconds if the output met it. If not, the model cannot either — and it will optimise for sounding finished.

Where this meets PD 1

The Mission line is where the six tests get enforced. A Mission that only names a topic produces a task a student can complete without thinking, however good the rest of the prompt is.

Add this to any Mission and the output changes: The task must not be completable by a student who still believes [state the misconception]. A student who has not understood [the concept] should get it visibly wrong. Then tell me, in one line, what a wrong answer would look like.

That last line is the check. If the model cannot describe a wrong answer, the task does not discriminate and it is not worth setting.

Activity — build the Engine

  1. In the Prompt Builder, write your Actor with one clause about what they are known for.
  2. Write the Mission, then apply the completion test aloud.
  3. Add the misconception clause above.
  4. Copy the prompt and run it. Keep the output for Module 3.

Check your understanding

What makes an Actor line useful rather than decorative?

Take a request you would normally type in one line. Rewrite the Mission so completion is checkable.

The move is converting an intention into a specification. 'Make a starter on fractions' becomes 'Write a five-minute starter with four items, where item three is answerable only by a student who understands that a fraction is one number rather than two — and tell me what a student holding that misconception would answer.'

Three things changed: a length, a count, and a discriminating item with a stated wrong answer. All three are checkable in seconds. The original was checkable only by feel, which is why it produced something that felt fine and taught nothing.

Module 3 — The Crucible

90 minutes · Outcome: you stop accepting first drafts, and you check what you would not have checked.

3.1  The first answer is not the answer

A model produces a plausible draft fast, and the speed is what makes it persuasive. The Crucible has two moves: make it iterate, then make it verify.

3.2  Iterate

PatternWhat you addWhen
Chain of thoughtThink step by step and show your reasoning before the final answer.Anything with a sequence, a calculation or a design decision you want to inspect.
RefinementPropose two sharper versions of this task and ask which I prefer before starting.When you are not yet sure what you want. Costs one exchange, usually saves three.
VerifierAsk me three clarifying questions, one at a time, before attempting this.The highest-value pattern for task design, and the one teachers use least.
Try the Verifier pattern once and you will keep it. The questions a model asks reveal what your brief left out — and the gaps it finds are usually the ones your students would have found in the lesson.

3.3  Verify

Five filters. Use one or two, not five — check the thing most likely to be wrong.

FilterWhat it asks forCatches
AssumptionsList every assumption you are making and rank each by confidence.Silent guesses about your class, curriculum or level.
SourcesCite two independent sources for each major claim, with URLs.Confident invention in factual content.
Counter-evidenceFind one credible source that disagrees, and explain why.Consensus framing where the field is contested.
AuditingRe-compute every figure and show your working.Arithmetic and data errors — common, and easily missed.
Cross-modelFlag anything a second model might dispute.Content that is stylistically confident and substantively shaky.
The Sources filter, honestly. It reduces invented citations; it does not eliminate them. Open the links. A fabricated URL is the most embarrassing thing that can reach a student and it takes twenty seconds to check.

Activity — put Module 2's output through the Crucible

  1. Take the output you kept from Module 2.
  2. Turn on the Verifier pattern in the Prompt Builder and re-run it. Answer its questions honestly.
  3. Compare the second output with the first. Note what changed.
  4. Apply the Assumptions filter. The low-confidence items are your lesson's risk register.
  5. If there is factual content, apply Sources and open every link.

Check your understanding

Why is the Verifier pattern especially useful for task design?

Which verification filter matters most in your subject, and why?

A good answer names one filter and ties it to a real failure mode. In history or geography it is usually Sources, because generated content invents plausible references and a fabricated citation in a student resource is unrecoverable. In mathematics and the sciences it is Auditing, because arithmetic slips are frequent, look right, and propagate through a worked example. In anything with a contested literature it is Counter-evidence, because the default register is consensus even where none exists.

A weak answer says all of them. Five filters on every prompt is a way of checking nothing carefully.

Module 4 — The Polish (OCEAN)

90 minutes · Outcome: output that does not read like every other generated resource — wired to a differentiation strategy and a check-in.

4.1  The five

LetterAddEffect
OriginalGive me three angles a typical teacher resource would not think of.Breaks the default framing, which is the most-used framing in the training data.
ConcreteBack every claim with one real, specific example.Kills abstraction, which is where generated content hides.
EvidentShow your logic in three bullets before the final answer.Makes the reasoning inspectable, so you can reject the route rather than the output.
AssertiveTake a clear stance rather than listing every option equally.Stops the balanced-list default that leaves you doing the deciding.
NarrativeWrite with a flow: hook, problem, insight, proof.Good for anything a student reads. Not for a rubric.
Two or three, not five. OCEAN is seasoning; all of it at once reads as strenuously written.

4.2  Wiring in the differentiation strategy

This is where PD 2, 3 and 4 meet the prompt. The builder carries the four Transforming Tasks strategies and their techniques; selecting one writes the instruction for you.

StrategyTechniques in the builder
Closed → openDifferent perspectives · many entry points · many pathways · many solutions
Tell → askSocratic questioning · explore before explain · use dialogue · student voice
Procedure → problem solvingStudents determine the problem · insufficient information · no steps given · irrelevant information
Information → understandingThe techniques that turn content into something students must do something with

Each strategy also links to the Department's published exemplars by subject, so you can see a before-and-after in your own discipline before writing the instruction.

4.3  Wiring in the check-in

Twenty formative assessment practices, grouped by when they happen — end of lesson, mid lesson, and during independent work. Selecting one adds it to the prompt, so the task arrives with its evidence method attached rather than bolted on afterwards.

This is the habit worth building: a task and the way you will read it are one design decision, not two.

The finished shape

A prompt built through all four stages is long, and length is not the point — specification is. Roughly what comes out:

MAP Grade 8 science, mid-unit on ecosystems, 50-minute periods. They can name a food chain but cannot say where the energy goes. Two students new to English. Attached: last week's lesson and the standard on energy flow. No search needed. AIM You are a veteran Grade 8 science teacher known for surfacing misconceptions early. Working from the attached lesson and standard, design a 50-minute lesson with a hook, one hands-on activity and a five-question exit ticket whose wrong options are real misconceptions. DIFF Use 'procedure to problem solving': give insufficient information and let students identify what they need. CHECK-IN Include a mini-whiteboard check at the midpoint. CRUCIBLE Ask me three clarifying questions first, one at a time. Then list your assumptions and rank them by confidence. OCEAN Be concrete — one real example per suggestion. Show your logic in three bullets before the final answer.

That is about ninety seconds of typing in the builder, and it replaces the twenty minutes you would spend fixing a generic first draft.

Activity — build three, keep one

  1. Build three complete prompts in the Prompt Builder for three tasks you are setting in the next fortnight.
  2. Run all three. Keep the outputs.
  3. Choose the one you would actually set, and say why the other two failed.
  4. Write down what you rejected from the winning output and edited by hand. That list is your skill developing.

Check your understanding

Why does the builder attach the check-in to the prompt rather than leaving it until afterwards?

Take one output you generated. What did you reject and edit by hand, and what does that tell you?

The rejection list is the clearest picture of your own expertise you will get. Common entries: a context that was plausible but not true of your school; a scaffold that removed the thinking rather than supporting it; an exit ticket whose wrong options were careless rather than diagnostic; a reading level right on average and wrong for two named children.

What it tells you is which part of the brief was underspecified — every rejection maps back to a MAP or Mission line you could have written. Over three or four prompts the list gets shorter, and that is the actual skill this course builds.
